Why this looks different from a typical "fraud heat map." No organization publishes a single 0–100 "real estate fraud score" per country — so rather than inventing one, this snapshot pulls the closest real, citable measures: the Basel AML Index (money-laundering & financial-crime structural risk), Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index and its real-estate-specific OREO Index, and FATF's typology reports on how real estate is actually used to launder money. Every figure below is sourced — see Section 4.

OREO index — real estate ownership opacity

Transparency International / Anti-Corruption Data Collective, March 2025 · 24 jurisdictions assessed

This is the only index of its kind built specifically around real estate: it scores jurisdictions on (1) how accessible real-estate ownership data is, and (2) how far anti-money-laundering rules actually cover the property sector. Of our 8 countries, 6 were assessed (Malaysia was not included). No jurisdiction scored full marks; 10 of the 24 scored below 5 out of 10.

🇬🇧 UK (England & Wales)
Scores highest of all 24 jurisdictions on the data-transparency pillar, alongside France — ownership records are relatively accessible to the public.
🇦🇪 UAE
One of several jurisdictions that introduced reforms after FATF scrutiny, but the index finds enforcement of those reforms still incomplete.
🇸🇬 Singapore
Some corporate beneficial-ownership data is recorded, but real estate ownership records themselves are not openly published.
🇮🇳 India / 🇮🇩 Indonesia
Both assessed among the 24; the index's public summary highlights gaps in real-estate data availability more broadly across G20 emerging markets rather than singling out either country.
🇺🇸 USA
Long criticized for allowing anonymous shell-company property purchases; FinCEN's 2024–25 real estate reporting rule is a direct policy response to this gap.
🇦🇺 Australia
Assessed in the index; separately, Australia only began extending AML/CTF obligations to real estate agents and other "Tranche 2" entities starting 2026.
04

Global fraud taxonomy — how real estate is actually abused

Generic typology, per FATF 2007 & 2022 real estate reports — deliberately not scored per country

Unlike the country matrix above, these categories are not given a per-country severity rating here, because no public dataset breaks real estate fraud down this granularly by country. FATF's own reports describe these as universal vulnerabilities of the sector, present to varying degrees everywhere it operates.

01
Cash purchases & complex financing
Buying property outright with cash, or through layered loans and credit structures that obscure the true source of funds.
02
Shell & nominee ownership
Holding title through companies, trusts, or nominees so the real beneficial owner is never recorded on any public register.
03
Under- or over-valuation
Manipulating the declared sale price — under-valuing to dodge tax, over-valuing to move extra money through the transaction.
04
Title & document fraud
Forged deeds, powers of attorney, or backdated agreements used to fabricate or transfer ownership unlawfully.
05
Wire & closing fraud
Interception of email or wire instructions during closing to redirect a buyer's deposit or final payment to criminals.
06
Multiple sale / double-selling
The same unit or plot sold to more than one buyer, common in off-plan and pre-construction markets with weak escrow controls.
07
Developer / project fund diversion
Buyer deposits or construction financing diverted away from the project they were raised for, leaving it stalled or abandoned.
08
Professional collusion
Agents, brokers, valuers or lawyers knowingly facilitating a transaction they know or suspect involves illicit funds.
09
Cross-border layering
Routing purchase funds through multiple jurisdictions and intermediary entities before they reach the property market.
10
Online listing & rental scams
Fake listings, phishing, or advance-fee scams targeting buyers and renters directly, a fast-growing cyber-enabled category.
